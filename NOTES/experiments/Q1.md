# Research question (Q1)

**RQ1.** *Do ball-tree branch-and-bound (BnB) searches identify central (near-center) query pairs **faster and more reliably** than strong alternatives (random sampling; dual kd-tree BnB à la NeurIPS’00), particularly as the candidate set size increases and central pairs become rarer (stricter centrality)?*

# Metric — Accuracy\@Budget (A\@B)

We evaluate one **anytime** metric, reported as two curves (time-budget and objective-call-budget):

Let

$$
d(i,j)=\frac{\big|\langle c,\ x_i-x_j\rangle-\tfrac12(\|x_i\|^2-\|x_j\|^2)\big|}{\|x_i-x_j\|}
$$

be the exact distance from the center $c$ to the mid-hyperplane of pair $(i,j)$.
Define the **oracle best** distance on the dataset:

$$
d^\star\ :=\ \min_{i<j} d(i,j),
$$

computed once per dataset/center using an **exact** BnB solver (sanity check: both the kd-tree and ball-tree exact BnB must return the same $d^\star$).

For a method under budget $B$, let $d_B$ be the **best distance found so far**. We report:

$$
\boxed{\ \text{A@B}\ :=\ \min\Big(1,\ \frac{d^\star}{\,d_B\,}\Big)\ }\qquad \text{(higher is better; 1.0 = optimal)}
$$

**Why this normalization (vs. earlier $r_{\max}$ scaling)?**

* It yields **dataset-comparable** accuracy: the ceiling is always 1 (achieving the true optimum $d^\star$), regardless of scale or geometry.
* It directly measures **optimality gap** (how close to the best possible pair you are) rather than absolute distance, which can vary across datasets.
* The NeurIPS’00 paper doesn’t define this task-specific metric; we use a standard **anytime optimality ratio**, with $d^\star$ verified by exact BnB.

# Dual kd-tree BnB (NeurIPS 2000) — objective & bounds (kept)

We instantiate Gray & Moore’s dual-tree framework with this **objective** and **node-pair bounds** (tree build is independent of $c$):

* For node $N$: projection interval $I_s(N)=[\min_{x\in N}\langle c,x\rangle,\ \max_{x\in N}\langle c,x\rangle]$; norm-squared interval $I_{r^2}(N)=[\min\|x\|^2,\max\|x\|^2]$.
* For node pair $(A,B)$:

  $$
  I_N = I_s(A)\ominus I_s(B)\ \ominus\ \tfrac12 I_{r^2}(A)\ \oplus\ \tfrac12 I_{r^2}(B),
  $$

  $\ |I_N|=[\min|I_N|,\max|I_N|]$, and box distances $D_{\min},D_{\max}$.

  $$
  \boxed{\ \text{LB}(A,B)=\frac{\min|I_N|}{D_{\max}+\varepsilon}\ ,\quad
  \text{UB}(A,B)=\frac{\max|I_N|}{\max(D_{\min},\varepsilon)}\ }
  $$
* Use best-first dual-tree traversal with **exclusion** (LB) and **subsumption** (UB), exactly in the NeurIPS’00 style.

# Experimental protocol (concise, with your budget scheme)

**Datasets & centers.**

* Fix dataset $R$. For each run, sample a **random center** $c$ (e.g., random data point or Gaussian draw rescaled to typical norm).
* Compute the oracle $d^\star$ once per $(R,c)$ using an **exact** BnB (both kd- and ball-tree implementations must match).

**Methods compared.**

1. **Random sampling (baseline)**
2. **Dual kd-tree BnB (NeurIPS’00)** with the objective/bounds above
3. **Ball-tree BnB** (your method)

**Budgets.**

* **Time budget (wall-clock).** For each $(R,c)$, run **both BnB methods to completion** (until they can certify optimality). Repeat timing several times; take

  $$
  T_{\max}=\max\{\text{recorded total times of the two BnB methods across repeats}\}.
  $$

  Evaluate at grid points $t \in \{0.05,0.10,\dots,1.00\}\,T_{\max}$. At each $t$, record $d_t$ (best distance found so far) and compute **A\@t**.
  *(Random runs until $T_{\max}$; log its best-so-far $d_t$ at the same checkpoints.)*

* **Objective-call budget.** Let $P_{\max}=n(n-1)/2$ (total pairs). Use checkpoints

  $$
  m \in \{0.05,0.10,\dots,1.00\}\,P_{\max}.
  $$

  Count **objective evaluations** (times an exact $d(i,j)$ is computed—not pruned). At each $m$, record $d_m$ and compute **A\@m**.

**Aggregation & plots.**

* For each budget axis, plot **median A\@B** with **95% bootstrap CIs** over $N$ runs (e.g., $N=50$).
* Report also the distribution of **finish times** (for the completion runs) to show absolute efficiency.

**Expected outcome for RQ1.**
Ball-tree BnB attains **higher A\@B** (closer to $1$) **earlier** on both axes, with gaps widening as $n$ grows or central pairs get rarer—evidence of superior pruning and reliability compared to random and the dual kd-tree baseline.
